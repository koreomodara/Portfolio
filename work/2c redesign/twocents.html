Case Study

My 2¢ Design 001 — Apple Music

A self‑initiated UX redesign focused on improving playback control clarity and queue management within Apple Music's desktop and mobile experience.

Role

UX Designer (solo project)

Timeline

2 weeks

Tools

Figma, FigJam, Apple Music, Spotify

Product Design Self‑Initiated Figma
Open full Figma file →

Overview

Apple Music has one of the most visually dynamic and immersive interfaces in music streaming — but underneath the surface, its playback controls and queue management create unnecessary friction that undermines the listening experience. This is a self‑initiated redesign focused on two specific problems: cryptic playback button states and a queue system that feels rigid and opaque rather than fluid and in your control. No new features, no visual overhaul — just fixing what's already there.

The Problem

Apple Music's playback controls — repeat, shuffle, autoplay, and automix — have multiple states with no in‑context explanation of what each one means. Pressing repeat once activates Repeat All; pressing it twice shows a "1" meaning Repeat This Song Only. The distinction is never communicated to the user, and on mobile there's no way to discover what these states mean without leaving the app entirely.

Queue management has a similar issue. "Add to Queue" only appears after a song is already in Play Next, with no explanation of that dependency anywhere in the interface. To a casual user the feature simply looks absent. Once a queue is built, there's no way to drag songs between the Queue and Continue Playing sections — two lists that represent one continuous listening experience but are treated as completely separate and static.

These aren't missing features. They're hidden ones. And hidden features are failed features.

Research & Discovery

Competitive Analysis

I compared Apple Music and Spotify across repeat/shuffle controls, autoplay behavior, and queue management:

  • Spotify surfaces hover tooltips on each playback button explaining exactly what it does in plain language — no documentation required
  • Spotify's autoplay starts with the same artist then branches outward into similar vibes; Apple Music jumps broadly within genre regardless of tempo or feel
  • Spotify's "Add to Queue" places songs directly into the next position without a Play Next dependency, though this collapses two distinct user intentions into one action
  • Spotify allows drag‑to‑reorder from the "Next from" section into the active queue, but not the reverse — bidirectional drag remains an unmet need across both platforms

Key insight: Spotify is clearer about controls but Apple Music's interface is more dynamic and visually compelling. The opportunity is to bring Spotify's clarity into Apple Music's experience without touching what already works.

User Insights

  • Repeat button states ("1" vs. standard repeat icon) are not intuitive — users have no way to discover what each state means without leaving the app
  • "Autoplay" and "Shuffle" are functionally similar but named differently, creating unnecessary confusion about what each mode actually does
  • "Add to Queue" is conditionally gated behind "Play Next" on both mobile and desktop with no explanation anywhere in the interface
  • Enabling shuffle clears the user's manually curated queue with no warning or confirmation — a destructive action with no recovery path visible to the user

Problem Statement

Apple Music offers a visually superior listening experience, but its playback controls and queue management create unnecessary confusion and friction — leaving users unsure of what mode they're in, what's coming next, and why they can't control it.

Design Principle

Don't change what people already know — just make it clearer. Every solution should feel like it was always supposed to be there.

Design Opportunities

1. Playback Control Clarity — adding contextual tooltips on desktop and a long‑press menu on mobile so users always understand what repeat and shuffle mode they're in, without leaving the app, reading documentation, or guessing.

2. Queue Management — this redesign surfaces a clear empty queue state that guides the user toward Play Next as the entry point, introduces drag‑to‑reorder within and across both Queue and Continue Playing sections, and treats them as one unified playback experience rather than two disconnected lists.

Design Process

  • Audit of current Apple Music experience across mobile and desktop — screenshots, annotation, and friction point documentation
  • Competitive analysis against Spotify across playback controls, queue management, and autoplay behavior
  • User flow mapping in FigJam covering three redesigned flows: playback controls, empty queue state, and drag to reorder
  • High‑fidelity designs within Apple Music's existing design language — 13 screens across home, now playing, queue, and playlist views
  • Annotated specs explaining the reasoning behind each design decision

Solutions

Playback Control Clarity

A long press on any playback button now reveals a contextual menu showing all available modes: Repeat Song, Repeat List, Shuffle, AutoPlay, and AutoMix. A checkmark indicates the active state. The buttons themselves remain unchanged — the muscle memory stays intact. The only addition is context delivered exactly when the user needs it.

Queue Management

An empty queue state now guides the user toward Play Next as the clear entry point, after which Add to Queue becomes visible and accessible. Songs can be dragged within and across Queue and Continue Playing — a song lifts with a shadow on long press, a highlighted drop zone appears on hover, and drops cleanly into the new position. The queue finally feels like something the user controls, not something that just happens to them.

Outcome & Reflection

This project started as a personal frustration — I kept pressing the repeat button and had no idea what the "1" meant. Two weeks later it became a full UX audit, competitive analysis, user flow documentation, and high‑fidelity redesign across 13 screens.

  • The best design insights come from using the product as a real user, not as a designer looking for problems
  • Fixing discoverability is often more impactful than adding new features — the solutions here already existed in the product, they just weren't findable
  • Designing within an established design system is its own skill — constraints force more creative and deliberate decisions than a blank canvas
